Methods and code · model-selection and validation guide

Choosing and validating ML potentials

From confined electrolytes to surface-response models.

In Project 2, I built and ran a CP2K–DP-GEN–DeePMD–LAMMPS workflow for NaCl–water in four carbon-nanotube diameters, with a check at every stage. This guide shows how I choose an interaction model, what it must pass before use, and what validated molecular results supply to larger-scale models. Applications to semiconductor surfaces are proposed and marked as such.

Working rule: a low force error is necessary but not sufficient. A benchmark of machine-learned force fields found that force accuracy does not reliably predict performance in simulation[1], so structure, stability and the transport or yield observable are tested directly.

How the pieces fit together: architecture, checkpoint, engine and workflow

The names in the table are architectures. Four different things are often called "the model":

Architecture
the functional form, for example DeepPot-SE or MACE.
Trained checkpoint
one set of fitted parameters, valid for the species, conditions and reference method behind its training data. Pretrained "foundation" checkpoints are no exception: their training domain and suitability are checked before use.
Software engine
the code that trains or evaluates a model: DeePMD-kit, MBX, LAMMPS.
Workflow
what decides the next calculation: DP-GEN in Project 2, the rule-based controller in the Continuum Lab.

The roles in this research:

  • CP2K: electronic-structure reference calculations, the energies and forces of selected configurations.
  • MLIP: an approximation to that reference energy landscape and its atomic forces.
  • LAMMPS: molecular dynamics with the chosen interaction model.
  • DP-GEN: concurrent learning, which cycles through exploration, labelling and training[9].
  • PINN: physics-constrained learning of solutions or closures[10]; here intended for the molecular-to-continuum connection (Project 3).
  • Continuum solver: fields and device-scale response from stated equations and closures (Continuum Lab).
  • Controller: selects and records computational actions according to stated rules and the available evidence; the Continuum Lab controller is rule-based and uses no language model.

The loop that connects these steps is described under Methods and code.

What an MLIP inherits from its reference

The reference method and its conditions

An MLIP can at best reproduce the method that labelled its data, and for water that method is a combination of choices. Early ab initio molecular dynamics with GGA functionals and classical nuclei found liquid water overstructured and glassy at ambient temperature and pressure; non-local dispersion counteracts the overstructuring and can change the liquid's structure and equilibrium density substantially[15][16]. With classical nuclei, the good agreement of dispersion-corrected revPBE-D3 has been attributed to a cancellation between the neglect of exact exchange and the neglect of nuclear quantum effects, so a functional's quality for water is judged with quantum nuclei rather than with an elevated temperature as a proxy[16]. A statement about one functional's structure or diffusion therefore needs its conditions: functional, dispersion treatment, nuclear treatment, temperature and density. A potential trained on such data inherits the combination. The strongest confined-water work chose its functional against higher-level benchmarks before training[17], and reference convergence (cutoffs, basis, SCF, grid) is part of the model's error budget, not a separate concern.

The Born–Oppenheimer picture

Nuclei move on the electronic ground-state surface. Ground-state charge redistribution, such as polarization or charge transfer between fragments, belongs to that surface: a local potential captures it only implicitly, through the environment inside its cutoff, whereas charge-equilibration potentials represent it explicitly, including non-local charge transfer[18]. Electronic excitation, nonadiabatic charge-state transitions such as ion neutralisation at a surface, and dissipative electronic friction lie outside a single ground-state surface and need dedicated methods. For water on carbon, a fluctuation-induced quantum friction between the liquid's charge fluctuations and the solid's electronic excitations has been proposed, a dissipative contribution absent from Born–Oppenheimer molecular dynamics by construction[19].

What MLIPs have delivered for confined water, ions and surfaces

Results from the primary literature, with the detail that makes each one credible. Nothing here is my own result; what my own work has used is marked in the model table.

Confined water and ions

  • Committee potentials with active learning made first-principles-quality simulation of water in carbon and boron nitride nanotubes, between MoS₂ sheets and at oxide interfaces routine: a committee of Behler–Parrinello networks, trained by adding the configurations on which the members disagree most, converged at roughly 300 reference structures per system[20].
  • Friction from first principles. With such potentials, water in carbon nanotubes shows roughly four to five times less friction than in boron nitride tubes, decreasing further in narrower tubes; the mechanism is attributed to oxygen motion on a curvature-smoothed energy landscape in carbon and to hydrogen–nitrogen docking and hopping in boron nitride[21].
  • A first-principles phase diagram of monolayer confined water, with the functional (revPBE0-D3) selected against quantum Monte Carlo before the potential was trained, revealed new monolayer ice phases, a hexatic-like phase and a superionic regime[17].

Semiconductor surfaces and plasma processes

  • Physical and reactive etching of silicon. A Deep Potential for Si–Cl–Ar was tested against experiment on sputtering yields, the depth of the amorphous–crystalline interface, etch yields versus flux ratio and ion energy, and the products of atomic layer etching[13]; for Si and SiO₂ ion-beam etching, neural-network potentials blended with the ZBL repulsion at short range were compared with experiment and classical MD[22].
  • Reactive chemistry on amorphous nitride. A neural-network potential for HF etching of amorphous Si₃N₄ reproduced the etching behaviour and was reduced to a continuum etch model[14], the step that feature-scale simulation needs.
  • Thin-film process steps such as atomic layer deposition of HfO₂ and plasma atomic layer etching of MoS₂ have been simulated with a moment tensor potential trained by active learning, with the stated expectation that the potential is reliable only within the process conditions sampled[23].
  • A benchmark for the field. Ten force-field models were compared on silicon nitride and hafnium oxide datasets with simulation indicators beyond energy and force errors[24]; the etching community's own roadmap asks for MD models tested more against experiment and extended to more materials, species and patterned surfaces[25].
Seven checks before using a potential

Each check answers a limitation documented in the literature. Together they are the reason this site measures a model's errors where they occur rather than averaging them away.

  1. Test behaviour in simulation, not only the force error. Models with excellent force scores can be unstable in molecular dynamics or give wrong structure and dynamics[1]; validation has to rest on predicted physical behaviour[11], and benchmarks are starting to score simulation indicators rather than forces alone[24]. Check: stability over the production length, structure against the reference, and the transport or yield observable itself.
  2. Check the reference before the model. A potential cannot be better than its labels: the functional, dispersion and nuclear treatment[15][16], the numerical settings and the convergence of the reference all pass into the model, and a minority species can carry a reference artefact that a pooled error never shows. Equal errors on training and test frames do not by themselves identify the cause: noisy labels, underfitting and representation limits can look alike. Check: a same-frame convergence ladder, reference consistency between training and test data, and errors resolved by species and environment (the reference check and per-species tooling of this site).
  3. Decide whether the observable needs long-range or electronic response. Local models carry electrostatics only inside the cutoff. Explicit long-range models represent charge in different ways, for example Gaussian charges at ionic and Wannier-centroid sites[6] or charge equilibration with non-local charge transfer[18], and both need labels beyond energies and forces. Whether a given model reproduces a conducting wall's screening response has to be tested for that model and wall; a conservative ground-state potential omits dissipative electronic friction in any case[19]. Check: whether the observable depends on long-range electrostatics, applied fields or the wall's electronic response and, if it does, a model-specific test of that response against the electronic-structure reference.
  4. Test whether committee disagreement tracks the error. Ensembles of neural-network potentials tend to report less uncertainty than their actual error, and the calibration differs between systems and architectures[26]. Active learning that selects by disagreement therefore finds unfamiliar configurations, not necessarily the wrong ones. Check: disagreement against independent reference errors, with the miss rate (large errors the committee did not flag) reported with its denominator.
  5. Test transfer outside the training conditions. A neural-network model that reproduced hydrogen bombardment of a crystalline diamond surface degraded on amorphous carbon and at higher impact energies[27]; process potentials are stated to be reliable only inside the sampled process conditions[23]; and a general-purpose foundation model is described by its authors as having qualitative and at times quantitative accuracy[28]. Check: held-out conditions (temperature, energy, composition, surface state) that were never in training, and a pretrained model's training domain before it is used.
  6. For energetic collisions, check the short-range physics. Thermal reference data seldom contain close approaches, so an energetic ion's repulsive wall is extrapolated unless a short-range potential such as ZBL is blended in[22]; the ion's charge state and its neutralisation lie outside a ground-state potential; and the decisive tests are sputter and etch yields against beam experiments[13], which classical force fields must pass too[29]. Check: the short-range treatment and its switching range, the energy range covered by labels, and yields with replica uncertainties.
  7. Check the transport estimator itself. Green–Kubo friction depends on the order of limits taken in a finite system[30]; in a finite tube or slit the running integral decays and the constrained-momentum estimator gives an effective, not an intrinsic, friction[12]; correlated frames from one trajectory leak between training and test sets; and a relaxation time computed from a disputed friction estimate is not independent validation. A short trajectory may not support a reliable uncertainty estimate; assess correlation, sampling length and independent replication. Check: estimator definitions, finite-size behaviour, independent replicas, group-wise data splits, and comparison with a driven measurement under matched conditions.
Evidence I look for before deployment

Before a potential is deployed, whether trained here or taken from a paper, I look for the following evidence; each item answers one or more of the checks above.

  1. The reference method, its numerical settings and a convergence test on the same frames; the functional's known biases for the system stated, with the dispersion and nuclear treatment.
  2. Errors per species and per environment with their tails, not one pooled number; the error definition (component or vector RMSE, MAE) and the counts behind it.
  3. Test data from configurations and runs the model never influenced, split by origin; the ancestry of every test frame.
  4. Stability in production-length dynamics from several starting structures, and structure (coordination, density profiles, g(r)) against the reference.
  5. The transport or yield observable with replica uncertainties, its finite-size behaviour, and a statement of what the error bar does not cover.
  6. For uncertainty claims: committee disagreement compared with independent reference errors, including the misses.
  7. For collisions: the short-range treatment, the label energy range, how the ion's charge was handled, and yields against beam data.
  8. A written scope: fields, ensembles, compositions, geometries and conditions the model may and may not be used for.
Biological inspiration: the mechanism sets the model requirements

Biological channels motivate the questions of hydration, coordination and selectivity. The demonstrations on this site concern water and electrolytes under carbon confinement.

Aquaporin-inspired questions

Water orientation, confinement and single-file water transport are questions of structure and dynamics. Proton transfer along a water chain is different: it breaks and forms bonds, so the model must be able to represent those bonding changes. A rigid classical water model cannot; a machine-learned potential can only where such configurations are in its reference data.

Potassium-channel-inspired questions

Selectivity is governed by hydration, coordination and transfer free energies: the model must describe an ion's water shell being replaced by pore-lining atoms as accurately as hydration in bulk water. An isolated binding energy does not determine selectivity, and a pore diameter alone does not establish that a continuum description is valid.

The biological mechanisms and their sources are summarised in the Continuum Lab's biological-inspiration notes.

Research opportunities and next tests
  • Electrostatics under conducting confinement. A local model represents a metallic or semimetallic wall's screening only implicitly, within its cutoff, and explicit long-range models represent charge in different ways[6][18]. Next test: for a given model and wall, compare the response to a nearby charged ion or an applied field with the electronic-structure reference.
  • Electronic friction. A conservative ground-state potential omits the dissipative electronic contribution. If fluctuation-induced quantum friction contributes to water–carbon friction[19], friction computed with such a potential contains only the Born–Oppenheimer contribution. Next test: report it as such, and compare it with measurements together with an estimate of the electronic contribution where one is available.
  • Calibrated uncertainty for transport. Committee spread tends to underestimate the actual error[26]; an uncertainty that propagates to a friction coefficient or a yield with a stated coverage is not yet standard practice. Next test: calibrate committee disagreement against independent reference errors, then propagate it to the transport observable.
  • Benchmarks that score the observable. Force-error leaderboards do not score simulation behaviour[1]; simulation-indicator benchmarks exist for semiconductor materials[24]. Next test: a confined-electrolyte benchmark scored on stability, hydration structure and transport.
  • Reference data for reactive processes. Generating relevant reference data for reactive etching is still described as a major challenge by a group that has done it[31], and the etching roadmap asks for more materials, species and patterned surfaces[25]. Next test: sputter and etch yields against beam data across the label energy range, with replica uncertainties.
