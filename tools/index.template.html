Research question

From molecular interactions to collective transport

When do individual molecular interactions matter, and when can we trust the average?

Transport in a fluid is the collective result of molecules interacting with one another and with surfaces. In channels only a few molecules wide, packing, correlations and the wall shape the flow; in wider channels, averaged fields and constitutive relations carry the same physics. I study where continuum descriptions hold, where they fail and which molecular details they must retain.

  • Collective transportpeer-reviewed articles · public CV

I combine molecular simulation, statistical mechanics and continuum modelling, and deliver results in the form a continuum solver uses: a wall law with a test of where it stops being reliable. Currently, molecular simulation supplies the wall physics, continuum theory carries the solution, and machine learning corrects only what the theory misses. Applications include membrane separation, nanofluidic devices and energy conversion, and interfacial thermal management; my longer-term aim is adaptive simulation.

Why I think across scales

From an ant to a colony, a person to a community and a star to a galaxy, I am fascinated by how interactions give rise to collective organisation. A single individual is hard to predict, yet the collective often moves in recognisable patterns. The mechanisms differ, but the question keeps returning: what must we understand about the parts to explain the whole? My research makes that question quantitative for molecular transport.

Project 1

Molecular structure, boundary conditions and nanoscale transport

Peer-reviewed research

Scientific question
Where does a confined fluid actually end at a nanoscale wall, and which continuum quantities, boundary conditions and transport laws still describe what the molecules do there?
My contribution
A first-law energy-balance definition of the flow boundary inside a carbon nanotube, tested against Hagen–Poiseuille limits (Phys. Rev. E 2021); a critical review with scale-effects analysis of water-transport estimates in nanotubes (Phys. Chem. Chem. Phys. 2024); and a study of molecular admission, packing and transport in subnanometre graphene slits (Phys. Chem. Chem. Phys., accepted 2026).
Available evidence
Phys. Rev. E 103, 053115 (2021) · Phys. Chem. Chem. Phys. 26, 19069 (2024) · Phys. Chem. Chem. Phys., accepted (2026)
Methods and next development

In Physical Review E (2021) I developed a first-law energy-balance analysis that locates the flow boundary for a simple liquid inside a single-walled carbon nanotube from non-equilibrium and equilibrium molecular dynamics, and tested the resulting boundary against Hagen–Poiseuille limits under controlled wall–fluid interaction. In Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics (2024) I led a critical review of experimental and simulation estimates of water transport in carbon nanotubes and a scale-effects analysis of how boundary definitions enter Hagen–Poiseuille-type estimates. In Physical Chemistry Chemical Physics (accepted, 2026) I examined how molecular admission and packing govern transport in subnanometre graphene slits.

Next development. Two further manuscripts from this work are in submission: one on finite-time diffusivity and wall–fluid interaction strength, one on hydraulic resistance and the limits of Navier-slip transferability in graphene nanoslits. The seven studies of the doctoral thesis are summarised under Publications, with full titles in the public CV.

Project 2

Error-aware MLIPs for CNT-confined NaCl–water

Current research

Scientific question
When can a machine-learned potential be trusted for transport in a nanotube, and how do you find out when it cannot?
My contribution
I built and ran the full workflow for NaCl–water in four carbon-nanotube diameters, with a check at every stage: CP2K ab initio MD seeds and reference labels (PBE-D3(BJ)), DP-GEN active learning, DeePMD-kit models and LAMMPS production runs.
What the checks showed
  1. Species-resolved validation. Per-species force checks on fresh DFT frames found one atom type much worse than the acceptable overall error suggested.
  2. Committee uncertainty assessment. Committee disagreement did not flag that error, and targeted extra reference data did not reduce it; its cause is being tested.
  3. Transport-estimator sensitivity. Two standard friction estimators gave different values, most strongly in the widest tube, so the friction results remain under analysis.
Available evidence
Ab initio MD snapshots (below the project cards) · trajectory inspector (interactive, all four tubes) · methods and software demonstration (synthetic example data, tests executed) · workflow schematic · friction results and error analysis are unpublished and shown on request.
Methods and next development

Periodic Gaussian-and-plane-wave reference calculations in CP2K (PBE-D3(BJ), GTH pseudopotentials, DZVP-MOLOPT basis sets, with Fermi–Dirac occupation smearing for SCF convergence of the metallic armchair tubes); selection of reference configurations by DP-GEN active learning with a DeePMD-kit committee; consistency checks on the reference data; element-resolved force errors (C, O, H, Na⁺, Cl⁻) with committee disagreement; structural diagnostics, stability testing and LAMMPS deployment within a declared scope. The model is energy-and-force only and local; its validity envelope is stated in the package's model-scope note.

Next development. Test whether the reference data, data coverage or the model type explains the species-specific error, then settle one friction estimator for all four tubes.

Project 3

PINN closure for wall friction and flow in nanoconfined water

Manuscript in preparation

Scientific question
In channels only a few molecules wide, the wall boundary condition can control the flow as strongly as the bulk equations do. Can a physically constrained model carry that wall physics into a closure a continuum solver can use, and show where it stops being reliable?
My contribution
This project combines molecular simulation, slip-flow theory and physics-informed neural networks (PINNs): an analytic solution carries the physics, and a network learns only the residual that the theory misses. Symmetries and the zero-forcing limit are built into the model's architecture rather than added as loss penalties, and models are scored only on geometries withheld from training.
Available evidence
Title and scope in the public CV (the closing study of the doctoral thesis); the molecular data come from the transport work of Project 1. Results are held until publication; a benchmark will be linked here when it is ready.
Methods and next development

The aim is a closure whose useful range and failure modes can be examined, rather than a fit that reproduces simulation data. Physics enters through the constraints imposed on the model, and the evaluation design separates training, validation and untouched evaluation conditions so that reported performance is not selected on the data that reports it.

Next development. A benchmark comparing a conventional closure, a data-driven baseline and the PINN on geometries withheld from training, with explicit checks on physical consistency and predictive uncertainty.

Can a simulation identify what to compute next?

My longer-term goal is adaptive simulation that uses physical diagnostics, model errors and uncertainty to guide where computational effort is most useful.

Research direction

The proposed cycle is deliberately plain: examine an error in the current prediction; investigate its source, whether reference data, the interaction model, sampling or the continuum description; select the one calculation most likely to resolve it; update the model; and evaluate the outcome on cases that were not used to make the decision. The first milestone is one bounded, human-reviewed decision cycle with its inputs, rationale, cost and outcome recorded.

  1. Examine an error
  2. Investigate its source
  3. Select a useful calculation
  4. Update the model
  5. Evaluate the outcome

Research in Practice

Explore the proposed workflow. This walkthrough explains the method behind Project 2; it does not report executed training or scientific outcomes. Select a step to read its purpose. Steps 2–4 link to the methods and software demonstration (synthetic example data).

Workflow steps

Define the target

Name the transport quantity the prediction is for (flux, selectivity, friction, diffusivity), the uncertainty it must reach and the computational budget. Everything downstream is judged against this target, not against generic accuracy.

Check the reference

Verify that the CP2K reference data are internally consistent: functional and basis settings, energy, force and virial conventions, cell and cutoff choices, and whether independent evaluation configurations exist. A model trained on inconsistent references inherits the inconsistency. Entry point: CP2K protocol parsing and compatibility checking (source).

Expose model errors

Resolve the potential's errors by species and environment (C, O, H, Na⁺, Cl⁻; bulk-like against wall-adjacent water), compare committee members, and test structural diagnostics and MD stability. Ask which of these errors can change the target quantity. Entry point: per-species force diagnostics and uncertainty tooling (source).

Choose the next calculation

Decide between adding reference calculations where the important errors live, extending sampling where statistics limit the target, or testing the transport closure. Record the reason, run it, and return to the reference check. Entry point: the transparent decision prototype (source).

Methods and software demonstration · synthetic example data

Runnable tooling with synthetic fixtures for the three steps above (public package v0.1.1). It demonstrates the software, not DFT accuracy, MLIP accuracy or physical transport, and contains no campaign numbers or models; the four AIMD position trajectories are shown separately in the Simulation Lab. The package's documented tests were executed for this site (13 passed, 0 failed) and the outputs are shown on the evidence page.

Additional research direction: nature-inspired transport strategies (proposed)

Can transport strategies evolved by living systems become testable physical design rules for nanoscale devices? Each cue below is mapped natural mechanism → design variable → simulation experiment → engineering objective. Proposed programme: none of these is a validated device prediction. Hover or focus a cue to see which capability it targets; open it for the mapping.

Nature's transport strategies

Mangrove rootssalt exclusion under suction
Natural mechanism
Root membranes pass water and exclude most ions while transpiration pulls the flow.
Design variable
Pore radius relative to hydrated-ion radius; dehydration free-energy barrier; surface charge and Debye-length ratio.
Simulation experiment
NEMD of NaCl solutions through sub-nanometre nanotubes and slits; ion-entry potentials of mean force; water flux against rejection.
Engineering objective
Ionic selectivity at a stated water permeability: the selectivity–permeability trade-off.
Plant transpirationevaporation-driven passive flow
Natural mechanism
Evaporation at nanoscale menisci creates capillary suction that drives flow without a pump and carries latent heat away.
Design variable
Meniscus curvature and pore radius (capillary pressure); evaporative flux against the hydraulic resistance of the network; contact angle.
Simulation experiment
NEMD of evaporation-driven flow in nanochannels with liquid–vapour interfaces; interfacial heat flux.
Engineering objective
Passive pumping flux and evaporative heat removal per unit area at zero applied pressure.
Electric eelstacked ion-selective cells
Natural mechanism
Thousands of series-stacked electrocytes convert ionic concentration gradients through selective channels into a large voltage.
Design variable
Membrane cation/anion selectivity (transference number), stack count, gradient magnitude, ionic conductance.
Simulation experiment
Ionic currents and selectivity in charged nanopores under concentration and potential gradients; molecular closures feeding a Nernst–Planck stack model.
Engineering objective
Open-circuit voltage and power density from salinity gradients; voltage-controlled transport.
Moth eyegraded interfaces, no abrupt mismatch
Natural mechanism
A sub-wavelength graded surface removes the abrupt impedance change that causes reflection. The transport analogue is the entrance and exit resistance of an abrupt channel mouth.
Design variable
Gradient length of wall–fluid interaction or wettability at the entrance relative to channel radius; entrance-resistance coefficient.
Simulation experiment
NEMD flow through pores with graded entrance interactions; entrance and exit hydraulic resistance against gradient length.
Engineering objective
Reduced interfacial resistance through graded interfaces. The optical origin is the analogy only; no photonic simulation is claimed.
